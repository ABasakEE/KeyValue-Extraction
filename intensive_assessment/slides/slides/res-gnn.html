This went against our hypothesis. On a random document split the graph model does well, and each part adds a little. Under the matched template-disjoint protocol it falls further than LiLT. Our explanation is that Delaunay neighbours depend on the layout. If a template puts values under their keys instead of beside them, the graph connects different words, and message passing pulls in the wrong context. LiLT leans more on its language model, so it holds up better. The NER-fused number is edge link F1, a different metric, which is why it has its own panel.
